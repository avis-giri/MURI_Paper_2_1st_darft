\begin{abstract}
The fraction of soil water that remains liquid below \SI{0}{\degreeCelsius}
governs the thermal, mechanical, and biogeochemical behavior of frozen ground,
and every established way of measuring it requires contact with the soil. We
show that this quantity is accessible to reflectance spectroscopy. Because
liquid water and ice have offset absorption features, a dual-band logarithmic
ratio is linear in the liquid fraction of pore water and invariant to albedo.
We derive the index from a three-phase radiative transfer treatment in which
the mineral matrix, the adsorbed liquid film, and the ice phase are represented
explicitly, using published optical constants that are held fixed rather than
fitted. Applied to archived field spectra of permafrost cores and active-layer
horizons at Poker Flat Research Range, interior Alaska, the index separates
frozen from unfrozen material at the specimen-group level ($p = \num{7e-5}$),
and a weaker band pair recovers the separation in saturated organic horizons
where the strong bands are opaque ($p = \num{1e-5}$). Propagating the forward
model across \num{17612} soil layers of a national laboratory library, the
predicted index change over the freeze-out interval exceeds a three-sigma
contact-probe threshold for \SI{97}{\percent} to \SI{99}{\percent} of layers,
with permafrost-order soils yielding the largest response. Twenty-three years
of soil temperature records near the site show that the ground at
\SI{5}{\centi\meter} spends all of its sub-zero time warmer than
\SI{-5}{\degreeCelsius}, so the interval where the retrieval is expected to
work and the interval the ground occupies coincide. A structured survey of
\num{9021} publications finds one work at the intersection of reflectance
spectroscopy and unfrozen water against \num{46.7} expected under independence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} amount of water that stays liquid in soil below the bulk
freezing point sets how frozen ground conducts heat, deforms under load, and
releases carbon on thaw. Its dependence on temperature is the soil freezing
characteristic curve. A compilation of \num{416} measured curves drawn from
\num{254} publications between 1921 and 2021 records methods based on nuclear
magnetic resonance, permittivity and time domain reflectometry, calorimetry,
dilatometry, and neutron scattering~\cite{Devoie_2022}. Every one of them
requires contact with the soil. The nearest approach from the measurement side
is dielectric spectroscopy, which estimates ice content in frozen porous media
from the permittivity spectrum~\cite{Bittelli_2004}. Geophysical imaging of the
same quantity through spectral induced polarization is established for
seasonally frozen ground~\cite{Maierhofer_2024,Wu_2017}, and dielectric mixing
models that parameterize unfrozen water underpin microwave remote sensing of
frozen soil~\cite{Mironov_2004,Bakian_Dogaheh_2025,Wu_2022,Wang_2024}.

Physical models of soil reflectance have matured in parallel. The MARMIT family
represents wet soil as a dry substrate beneath a water film whose thickness
sets the strength of the water absorption
features~\cite{Bablet_2018,Dupiau_2022}, two-flux theory yields transformed
reflectances linear in water content in the shortwave
infrared~\cite{Sadeghi_2015}, and the Hapke formulation has been extended
across the spectrum to characterize soil physical properties~\cite{Ding_2022}.
These strands were recently unified into a single model spanning dry and wet
conditions~\cite{Ding_2026}. That model, like every predecessor, treats water in
one phase only, and its systematic validation covers \num{219} soil samples.
The soil radiative transfer literature therefore stops at the temperature where
frozen ground becomes interesting, and the freezing literature stops on the
other side of the same boundary.

\subsection{A structured survey of the surrounding literature}

To test whether that impression survives contact with the record, we assembled
\num{9021} works from ten topic queries spanning soil spectroscopy, freezing
curves, permafrost remote sensing, soil radiative transfer, geoelectrical
methods, and pedotransfer functions, and cross-tabulated sensing modality
against soil state over titles and abstracts. Fig.~\ref{fig:kg} shows the
result. Reflectance spectroscopy is the largest modality in the corpus
(\num{1833} works) and unfrozen water is an active state with \num{230} works,
yet their intersection contains a single publication against \num{46.7}
expected under independence, the largest proportional deficit in the matrix.
The corresponding cell for mid-infrared spectroscopy is empty. The same column
is well occupied by calorimetry and magnetic resonance (\num{40} works),
dielectric methods (\num{27}), and resistivity (\num{15}).

The deficit is not explained by lack of interest. Work on unfrozen water grew
from seven publications per five-year interval in 2000 to \num{105} in the
interval beginning 2020, while the optical count stayed at zero throughout, and
recent reviews document an active effort to predict the quantity from index
properties by statistical means~\cite{Li_2026,Ren_2023}. Nor is the deficit
explained by optical spectroscopy being immature: the same corpus shows it
already accounts for \SI{53}{\percent} of the carbonate literature,
\SI{49}{\percent} of cation exchange capacity, \SI{42}{\percent} of soil pH,
and \SI{39}{\percent} of soil organic carbon, in keeping with the large
laboratory calibration efforts of the past
decade~\cite{Wijewardane_2018,Dangal_2019,Safanelli_2025}. Where it is absent
is precisely the physical state variables of frozen ground, namely ice content
(\num{0} of \num{169} works), unfrozen water (\num{1} of \num{179}), and
thermal conductivity (\num{1} of \num{98}).

Section~\ref{sec:sensors} offers a mechanical explanation. The multispectral
satellites that dominate land remote sensing carry no bands in the spectral
regions where the liquid and ice features differ, so the measurement has been
inaccessible to the instruments most investigators use.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A three-phase forward model of soil reflectance in which the mineral
        matrix, the adsorbed liquid film, and the ice phase are represented
        explicitly, extending the wet-soil radiative transfer lineage across
        the phase boundary at which it currently terminates.
  \item A phase-partition index derived from that model, linear in the liquid
        fraction of pore water and invariant to albedo, defined in three band
        pairs whose differing absorption strengths suit dry, moist, and
        saturated organic soils.
  \item Evidence for the index in archived field spectra that were collected
        for an unrelated purpose, together with a demonstration of the
        surface-melt confound that limits uncontrolled measurements.
  \item An applicability analysis over \num{17612} layers of a national soil
        library, and an instrument-requirements analysis identifying which
        sensors can measure the index and which band pair survives atmospheric
        transmission.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Physical Basis}
\label{sec:physics}

\subsection{Optical contrast between liquid water and ice}

Liquid water and ice differ in their optical constants throughout the
reflective infrared~\cite{Warren_2008}. The liquid combination bands near
\SI{1450}{\nano\meter} and \SI{1940}{\nano\meter} shift and sharpen toward the
crystalline ice features near \SI{1500}{\nano\meter} and
\SI{2020}{\nano\meter}. Table~\ref{tab:alpha} lists the absorption coefficients
at the wavelengths used below, computed as $\alpha = 4\pi k / \lambda$ from the
published compilation for ice~\cite{Warren_2008} and the corresponding
compilation for liquid water. Within each pair the ordering of liquid and ice
absorption reverses, which is the property the index exploits.

\subsection{Three-phase forward model and the index}

Following the multilayer treatment of wet soil~\cite{Bablet_2018,Dupiau_2022},
we represent the illuminated volume as a dry substrate of reflectance $R_d$
overlain by a film of total water of equivalent thickness $L$, and we let the
film be a mixture of liquid and ice. Writing $f$ for the liquid fraction of the
pore water along the photon path, the effective absorption coefficient at
wavelength $\lambda$ is
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda,f) = f\,\alpha_w(\lambda) + (1-f)\,\alpha_i(\lambda),
\label{eq:mix}
\end{equation}
and a two-pass attenuation through the film gives
\begin{equation}
R(\lambda,f) = R_d(\lambda)\,\exp\!\left[-2\,\alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda,f)\,L\right].
\label{eq:refl}
\end{equation}
For a pair of wavelengths $(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)$ the logarithmic band ratio
\begin{equation}
I(f) = \ln\frac{R(\lambda_2,f)}{R(\lambda_1,f)}
\label{eq:index}
\end{equation}
removes the substrate term whenever $R_d$ is spectrally flat between the two
bands, and substituting~\eqref{eq:mix} and~\eqref{eq:refl} yields
\begin{equation}
I(f) = \ln\frac{R_d(\lambda_2)}{R_d(\lambda_1)}
  - 2L\left[\alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda_2,f) - \alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(\lambda_1,f)\right],
\label{eq:expanded}
\end{equation}
which is exactly linear in $f$ with slope
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial I}{\partial f} = 2L\left[(\alpha_i - \alpha_w)_{\lambda_2}
                                       - (\alpha_i - \alpha_w)_{\lambda_1}\right].
\label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}
Linearity in the retrieved quantity, and cancellation of the substrate term,
are the reasons to prefer the ratio over a band depth. The slope scales with
the film thickness $L$, which is why the analysis below reports results across
a fourfold range of $L$.

\subsection{Three band pairs}

Equation~\eqref{eq:slope} and Table~\ref{tab:alpha} define a natural tiering.
The pair at \num{1450} and \SI{1500}{\nano\meter} gives the largest slope and
is the primary form of the index. For a film of \SI{0.1}{\milli\meter} and a
freeze-out from all liquid to one quarter liquid it produces relative
reflectance changes of \SI{22}{\percent} to \SI{33}{\percent}. The pair at
\num{1940} and \SI{2020}{\nano\meter} is stronger still but saturates once $L$
exceeds about \SI{0.2}{\milli\meter}. The pair at \num{1200} and
\SI{1250}{\nano\meter} is roughly twenty times weaker and remains unsaturated
in saturated organic soils where the other pairs are opaque. The pair at
\num{970} and \SI{1030}{\nano\meter}, which the visible and near-infrared
literature might suggest, produces less than \SI{1}{\percent} change at
film-scale path lengths and is not usable.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Field spectra and characterized cores}

We acquired field measurements at Poker Flat Research Range (\ang{65.12}~N,
\ang{147.43}~W), interior Alaska, in a zone of discontinuous permafrost. The
active-layer dataset comprises \num{285} spectra over \num{42} site and depth
groups at seven sites, recorded with a PSR+ 3500 spectroradiometer spanning
\SIrange{350}{2500}{\nano\meter} in \num{2151} channels, in standoff geometry
over freshly exposed soil faces at fixed depth intervals. We acquired a separate set of
\num{95} spectra on frozen core material from three deep boreholes, logging the
sample temperature at each scan, spanning
\SI{-14.7}{\degreeCelsius} to \SI{1.2}{\degreeCelsius} over \num{18} site and
depth groups. The cores are characterized under an established
cryostratigraphic protocol~\cite{Iwahana_2014}, and \num{406} logged intervals
across ten boreholes carry gravimetric water content, volumetric ice content,
dry and wet density, saturation, organic and carbonate loss on ignition, pH,
conductivity, stable water isotopes, grain size, and cryostructure.

\subsection{National laboratory library}

We drew laboratory spectra and paired properties from an open soil spectral
library~\cite{Safanelli_2025}. The mid-infrared subset used here comprises
\num{76813} layers grouped into \num{20716} pedons. Restricting to layers that
carry both water retention endpoints at \SI{33}{\kilo\pascal} and
\SI{1500}{\kilo\pascal} together with clay and organic carbon gives \num{17612}
layers across \num{3708} pedons, of which \num{116} layers in \num{35} pedons
fall in the permafrost order. For comparison we assembled a parallel visible and
near-infrared set of \num{23647} spectra spanning the same
\SIrange{350}{2500}{\nano\meter} interval as the field instrument.

\subsection{Independent freezing curves}

For external comparison we used a compilation of \num{416} measured curves and
\num{869202} points~\cite{Devoie_2022}. Quality control requiring at least eight points, a warmest point above
\SI{-0.2}{\degreeCelsius}, and coverage colder than \SI{-3}{\degreeCelsius}
leaves \num{195} curves. Only ten of the \num{416} are silt-rich or organic,
and none of the silt-rich entries records an organic content, so the
compilation is thin in exactly the material this study targets.

\subsection{Ground thermal regime}

We obtained soil temperature and moisture records from the monitoring station at
Little Chena Ridge, \SI{32}{\kilo\meter} from the field site, comprising
\num{5469} daily values at \SI{5}{\centi\meter} depth from 2003 to 2026 with
companion series at \num{20} and \SI{51}{\centi\meter}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Index computation and quality control}

The index follows~\eqref{eq:index} with reflectance averaged over
\SI{\pm5}{\nano\meter} about each band center. Spectra are rejected when any
channel in the continuum interval falls at or below \SI{0.05}{\percent}
reflectance or exceeds \SI{100}{\percent}, both of which indicate detector
saturation or a failed reference rather than a physical measurement.

\subsection{Unit of independence}

Each specimen contributes several spectra and each site and depth group several
replicates, so the group rather than the spectrum is the unit of statistical
independence. We compare group medians throughout, and every permutation
test shuffles group labels. We registered this protocol before running any
comparison.

\subsection{Two-anchor reconstruction}

To quantify how much of a freezing curve the two laboratory retention endpoints
determine, each endpoint is mapped to a temperature through the Clapeyron
relation, whose coefficient $\rho_w L_f / T_0$ evaluates to
\SI{1.223}{\mega\pascal\per\kelvin}. This places \SI{33}{\kilo\pascal} at
\SI{-0.027}{\degreeCelsius} and \SI{1500}{\kilo\pascal} at
\SI{-1.227}{\degreeCelsius}, consistent with the similarity between freezing
and retention curves established by earlier
work~\cite{Koopmans_1966,Spaans_1996}. We solve a van Genuchten form exactly
through both anchors and score it against the measured points, separately inside
the bracketed interval and beyond it.

\subsection{National applicability}

For each library layer the liquid fraction remaining at the cold anchor is
$f = \theta_{1500} / \theta_{33}$, so the freeze-out across the bracketed
interval involves no extrapolation. The predicted index change follows
from~\eqref{eq:slope} with film thickness scaled by volumetric water content at
field capacity and calibrated so that the median mineral soil corresponds to
\SI{0.1}{\milli\meter}. We assess detectability against a three-sigma
threshold at a contact-probe noise level and, separately, at the replicate noise
measured in the field spectra.

\section{Preliminary Results}

\subsection{The index separates frozen from unfrozen material}

Applied to the archived Poker Flat spectra, the primary index gives a median of
\num{0.062} over \num{36} unfrozen active-layer groups and \num{-0.264} over the
seven core groups at or below \SI{-2}{\degreeCelsius}, a separation significant
at $p = \num{7e-5}$ by group-level permutation. The ordering is the one the
forward model predicts. Core groups measured near \SI{-1}{\degreeCelsius} fall
on the liquid side, which is expected behavior rather than a failure, since most
pore water in silt remains liquid at that temperature.

Two further results bear on the method's limits. A saturated surface peat
horizon at \SI{-12.5}{\degreeCelsius} reads on the liquid side under the primary
index, the opacity failure the forward model anticipates at high water content,
and the weak band pair recovers it at \num{-0.043}. Across all groups the weak
pair separates unfrozen from frozen material at $p = \num{1e-5}$ while passing
quality control on every replicate. Separately, spectra of exposed thaw faces in
the field are statistically indistinguishable from unfrozen horizons
($p = \num{0.82}$), which demonstrates in data the surface-melt confound that
prevents uncontrolled field measurements from resolving the phase partition.

\subsection{Retention endpoints are predictable from laboratory spectra}

Under pedon-grouped ten-fold cross-validation with nested component selection,
partial least squares regression predicts the cold retention endpoint at
$R^2 = 0.78 \pm 0.03$ and the warm endpoint at $R^2 = 0.56 \pm 0.13$. The
difference between random and grouped splits is \num{0.01} and \num{0.03}
respectively, so leakage is negligible in this dataset. The endpoint that
controls the freezing interval is the one predicted well.

\subsection{Two anchors nearly determine the curve}

Across \num{60} measured curves, a van Genuchten form solved through the two
anchors reconstructs the freezing curve beyond the bracketed interval with a
normalized root mean square error of \num{0.024} when the shape parameter is
correct, matching the \num{0.025} achieved by a free fit to all points. With a
fixed default shape parameter the error is \num{0.087}, and across the range of
shape parameters consistent with the anchors the worst case is \num{0.230}.
Organic soils are more sensitive than mineral soils, at \num{0.150} against
\num{0.068}. The shape parameter is therefore the single quantity the two
anchors fail to determine.

\subsection{The index is detectable across the national soil population}

Propagating the forward model across the \num{17612} library layers, the
predicted index change over the freeze-out interval exceeds a three-sigma
contact-probe threshold for \SI{97}{\percent} to \SI{99}{\percent} of layers,
and this conclusion is stable across a fourfold variation in the assumed film
thickness. Permafrost-order soils give the largest response, with a median index
change of \num{0.60} against \num{0.35} for the population at a reference film
thickness, because they freeze a larger share of their field-capacity water
within the bracketed interval, \SI{67}{\percent} against \SI{48}{\percent}
nationally. At the replicate noise measured in standoff field spectra the
detectable fraction falls to between \SI{0}{\percent} and \SI{43}{\percent},
which establishes controlled illumination as a requirement rather than a
convenience.

The corresponding resolution follows from~\eqref{eq:slope}. At contact-probe
noise the minimum detectable change in liquid fraction is \num{0.05} at three
sigma for the median soil, equivalent to
\SI{0.017}{\cubic\centi\meter\per\cubic\centi\meter} of volumetric water.

\subsection{The retrievable interval is the interval the ground occupies}

At \SI{5}{\centi\meter} depth the ground near the field site is below
\SI{0}{\degreeCelsius} on \SI{46}{\percent} of days across the record, yet its
coldest observed value is \SI{-5.6}{\degreeCelsius}. All of its sub-zero time is
warmer than \SI{-5}{\degreeCelsius} and \SI{70}{\percent} falls between
\SI{0}{\degreeCelsius} and \SI{-1.23}{\degreeCelsius}, the interval the
laboratory retention endpoints already bracket. Fig.~\ref{fig:conv} places the
three constraints side by side. The interval in which the physics predicts the
strongest signal, the interval the national retention measurements determine,
and the interval the ground actually occupies are the same interval.

\section{Instrument Requirements}
\label{sec:sensors}

The band pairs fall in different atmospheric regimes. The bands at \num{1450}
and \SI{1940}{\nano\meter} lie inside strong atmospheric water vapor absorption,
so the primary and the strongest pairs are laboratory and contact measurements.
Both bands of the weak pair at \num{1200} and \SI{1250}{\nano\meter} lie in a
clean atmospheric window, which makes it the form of the index available from
airborne and orbital platforms, at the cost of roughly a twentyfold reduction in
slope.

Sensor coverage is equally decisive. Sentinel-2 carries no band between
\num{945} and \SI{1374}{\nano\meter} and none between \num{1374} and
\SI{1610}{\nano\meter}, and Landsat 8 and 9 carry none between \num{870} and
\SI{1610}{\nano\meter}. Neither can sample any of the three pairs. The
measurement requires contiguous imaging spectroscopy, which is available from
airborne sensors and from current orbital imaging spectrometers. This offers a
mechanical explanation for the deficit in Fig.~\ref{fig:kg}: the instruments
that dominate land remote sensing are blind to the contrast.
