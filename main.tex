\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} amount of water that remains liquid in soil below the
bulk freezing point is the state variable that governs how frozen ground
conducts heat, deforms under load, and releases carbon on thaw. The
relationship between that unfrozen water content and temperature is the soil
freezing characteristic curve. It is a required input to essentially every
permafrost thermal model, and it is measured almost exclusively by methods
that must be inserted into the soil: nuclear magnetic resonance, time domain
reflectometry, capacitance probes, and calorimetry \cite{Devoie_2022}.

%% The argument of the paper, in the order a reviewer will test it.
%% 1. The quantity matters and is hard to measure.
%% 2. Optical remote sensing has never measured it, and the reason is that
%%    the soil radiative transfer literature stops at the freezing point.
%% 3. The physics says it should be measurable, because liquid water and ice
%%    have distinct and offset absorption features in the reflective infrared.
%% 4. We build the forward model, invert it, and bound the regime where the
%%    retrieval is trustworthy.

Physical models of soil reflectance have matured considerably in the solar
domain. The MARMIT family represents wet soil as a dry substrate overlain by
a water film whose thickness sets the strength of the water absorption
features \cite{Bablet_2018,Dupiau_2022}, two-flux theory yields transformed
reflectances that are linear in water content in the shortwave infrared
\cite{Sadeghi_2015}, and the Hapke formulation has been extended across the
spectrum to characterize soil physical properties \cite{Ding_2022}. These
strands were recently unified into a single model spanning dry and wet
conditions \cite{Ding_2026}. That unified model, like every predecessor,
treats water in one phase only, and its validation covers 219 soil samples.
The soil radiative transfer literature therefore stops precisely at the
temperature where frozen ground becomes interesting.

The freezing literature stops on the other side of the same boundary. A
compilation of 416 measured freezing curves drawn from 254 publications
between 1921 and 2021 records methods based on magnetic resonance,
permittivity, calorimetry, dilatometry, and neutron scattering, and it
contains no optical or infrared technique \cite{Devoie_2022}. The nearest
approach from the measurement side is dielectric spectroscopy, which
estimates ice content in frozen porous media from the permittivity spectrum
\cite{Bittelli_2004}. Geophysical imaging of the same quantity through
spectral induced polarization is now established for seasonally frozen ground
\cite{Maierhofer_2024,Wu_2017}, and dielectric mixing models that
parameterize unfrozen water underpin microwave remote sensing of frozen soil
\cite{Mironov_2004,Bakian_Dogaheh_2025}. An optical counterpart to that body
of work does not exist.

%% Guard the claim. Say optical or infrared, never "spectroscopic", because
%% dielectric spectroscopy is in the freezing-curve canon (Bittelli 2004).

The physical basis for one does exist. Liquid water and ice have distinct
optical constants throughout the reflective infrared \cite{Warren_2008}, and
their absorption features are offset: the liquid combination bands near
\SI{1400}{\nano\meter} and \SI{1900}{\nano\meter} shift and sharpen toward
the crystalline ice features near \SI{1500}{\nano\meter} and
\SI{2020}{\nano\meter}, with a further diagnostic ice band near
\SI{1030}{\nano\meter}. Band position and depth in this region therefore
carry information about how water is partitioned between phases, not only
about how much water is present. Laboratory reflectance studies of ice and
regolith mixtures in the planetary literature confirm that the partition is
spectrally expressed, although those experiments use mineral simulants and do
not resolve unfrozen water or a freezing curve \cite{Yoldi_2021}.

This paper makes three contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A three-phase forward model of soil reflectance in which the mineral
        matrix, the adsorbed liquid film, and the ice phase are represented
        explicitly, extending the wet-soil radiative transfer lineage across
        the phase boundary at which it currently terminates.
  \item A retrieval of unfrozen water content as a function of temperature
        from reflectance alone, benchmarked against a co-measured contact
        method on the same specimens, and against the published compilation
        of measured freezing curves.
  \item A bound on the retrievable regime. We report the temperature range
        and the soil classes over which the retrieval is trustworthy, and we
        state where the residual liquid films become too thin to express
        themselves in reflectance.
\end{enumerate}

equation, so that the depression of the freezing point plays the role of
matric potential \cite{Koopmans_1966,Spaans_1996}. Parametric forms fitted to
measured curves are widely used \cite{Kozlowski_2007}, and recent work has
concentrated on faster measurement \cite{Tian_2023} and on freezing point
depression in permafrost soils specifically \cite{Chuvilin_2022}.
