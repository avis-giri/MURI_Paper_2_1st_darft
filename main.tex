\section{Main idea}

When soil freezes, not all of its water turns to ice. A fraction stays liquid,
held in thin films on soil particles, and that fraction shrinks as the
temperature drops. How much stays liquid at a given temperature is called the
soil freezing characteristic curve. It matters because that remaining liquid
water is what carries heat, allows the ground to settle under a building or a
runway, and lets microbes keep releasing carbon in frozen soil.

Measuring it today means putting an instrument into the soil: a probe, a
calorimeter, or a magnetic resonance sensor. Our idea is that light can measure
it instead. Liquid water and ice absorb infrared light at slightly different
wavelengths. Liquid water absorbs most strongly near \SI{1450}{\nano\meter},
while ice absorbs most strongly near \SI{1500}{\nano\meter}. So if we measure
reflectance at both wavelengths and take the ratio, the result tells us how the
water is split between liquid and ice, without touching the sample.

I have now checked this end to end, and the answer each time was favourable. The
physics predicts a signal far above measurement noise. The signal is already
visible in the spectra we collected at Poker Flat for the previous paper. The
measurement does not appear in the published literature. And the whole analysis
runs as one reproducible pipeline, so every number in this document regenerates
from the raw data on demand.

What is missing is the laboratory work, and that is the only part still
outstanding. It can start within weeks: the soil samples collected last year are
already at SLU, have been measured with the PSR+ spectroradiometer, and have
never been scanned with the HySpex. The rest of this document sets out what I
found, what we already hold, and what I think we should do next.

\section{Why this has not been done before}

Two reasons:

First, the instruments. Sentinel-2 and Landsat are the satellites most soil and
land studies rely on. Sentinel-2 has no spectral band between \num{945} and
\SI{1374}{\nano\meter}, and none between \num{1374} and
\SI{1610}{\nano\meter}. Landsat has none between \num{870} and
\SI{1610}{\nano\meter}. The wavelengths where liquid water and ice differ fall
inside those gaps. Anyone working with these satellites simply cannot see the
effect. It takes an imaging spectrometer, which measures hundreds of contiguous
narrow bands rather than a handful of wide ones.

Second, the two research communities sit on opposite sides of the freezing
point. Soil spectroscopy has built large reflectance libraries, but for dried,
room-temperature samples. Frozen-ground science measures unfrozen water
carefully, but with contact sensors. A published compilation of freezing curves
measured between 1921 and 2021 lists every method the field uses, and all of
them require contact with the soil~\cite{Devoie_2022}.

I also checked the nearest neighbours carefully, because they define what we
can and cannot claim. Over snow, people have retrieved the three phases of
water with imaging spectrometers since 2006, and that machinery now runs in an
operational satellite chain~\cite{Green_2006,Bohn_2020}. Over planetary
surfaces, laboratories have measured ice and mineral mixtures in our exact
wavelengths for four decades~\cite{Clark_1981,Yoldi_2021}. What none of them
does is retrieve the liquid fraction coexisting with ice in soil, or connect
any of it to unfrozen water content and the freezing curve. The oldest of
those papers actually works in our favour: Clark showed in 1981 that the
absorption bands of water bound to minerals hold their positions as
temperature drops and stay visible even with ice present, which is exactly the
stability our measurement needs. And the snow methods rest on an assumption
that fails over soil, a soil background that is linear in wavelength, which is
the problem the four-wavelength form below is built to solve.

\section{What the measurement looks like}

Represent the wet soil surface as dry soil covered by a thin film of water. Some
of that film is liquid and some is ice. Light passes through the film, reflects
off the soil, and passes back out, so it is absorbed twice on the way. Writing
$f$ for the fraction of the film that is still liquid, the reflectance at a
wavelength $\lambda$ is
\[
R(\lambda,f)=R_{\mathrm{dry}}(\lambda)\,
\exp\!\left[-2L\left(f\,\alpha_w(\lambda)+(1-f)\,\alpha_i(\lambda)\right)\right],
\]
where $L$ is the film thickness and $\alpha_w$ and $\alpha_i$ are how strongly
liquid water and ice absorb. Taking the natural logarithm of the ratio of two
wavelengths cancels the dry soil term, and what remains is a straight line in
$f$. That is the useful property: the quantity we want appears linearly, and the
soil colour underneath drops out.

Table~\ref{tab:tiers} gives the three usable wavelength pairs. Within each pair
the absorption ordering flips between liquid and ice, which is what makes the
ratio informative. Which pair is usable depends on how the spectrum was
measured, because the atmosphere absorbs strongly at \SI{1450}{\nano\meter} and
\SI{1940}{\nano\meter}. Under sunlight those wavelengths arrive at the ground
already depleted, so only the \num{1200} and \SI{1250}{\nano\meter} pair is
reliable outdoors. In the laboratory, where the lamp is a metre from the sample,
all three are available.

For a silt freezing from fully liquid to one quarter liquid, the laboratory pair
predicts a change in reflectance of \SI{22}{\percent} to \SI{33}{\percent},
which is far above measurement noise.

Two effects complicate this, and both are now quantified. The index is
proportional to the distance light travels through the water film, and freezing
changes that distance by expanding the sample and forming ice lenses, so a
change in structure can imitate a change in phase. Adding a third measurement
at \SI{1650}{\nano\meter}, outside the absorption features, gives a form of the
index in which the path length cancels. Separately, liquid water absorbs
slightly differently as it cools even without freezing, worth about
\SI{3}{\percent} of the full range for every ten degrees. Both are second-order
against a signal that spans the full range, but the experiment measures three
wavelengths rather than two so that we can correct for them.

\section{The signal is already in our Poker Flat data}

I applied this to spectra collected for the previous paper, which were never
taken with this question in mind. Those spectra were measured at canopy level
under sunlight, with the instrument held above the soil rather than touching it,
so the atmospheric absorption regions are noisy. That decides which pair to
trust: the outdoor pair sits in a clean part of the spectrum and every sample
group passes quality control, while the laboratory pair is degraded and only a
fifth of the individual spectra survive screening.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
\item \textbf{Outdoor pair.} Unfrozen active-layer horizons give a median index
      of \num{0.055}, frozen cores \num{0.014}. Every sample group passes
      screening, and the two populations separate with a probability of
      \num{1e-5} of arising by chance. This is the result to put weight on.
\item \textbf{Laboratory pair.} The same comparison gives \num{0.062} against
      \num{-0.264}, separating at \num{7e-5}, but on a fifth of the spectra
      because the rest fail screening in the atmospheric absorption region.
\item \textbf{The two agree.} Across the core groups where both can be
      computed, the two indices track each other with a correlation of
      \num{0.75}. Since one sits in a clean part of the spectrum and the other
      does not, that agreement is what convinces me the laboratory-pair signal
      is real rather than atmospheric noise.
\item One saturated peat horizon behaves as the model predicts it should fail:
      the soil is so wet that the strong pair goes dark. The outdoor pair
      recovers it.
\item Spectra taken on exposed thaw faces in a soil pit look identical to
      unfrozen soil. A thin melted skin hides the frozen interior. This is the
      main reason the measurement has to be made under controlled conditions
      rather than in the field.
\end{itemize}

The practical consequence is that sunlight caps what the strongest pair can do.
Controlled illumination is not a convenience for the experiment, it is what
makes the laboratory pair available at all.

I also tested how far this would generalise using a public laboratory library
of soils from across the United States. For \SI{97}{\percent} to
\SI{99}{\percent} of the \num{17612} soil layers that have both spectra and
water-retention measurements, the predicted signal is large enough to detect.
Permafrost-order soils give the largest signal of any soil group. The implied
precision is about \SI{0.017}{\cubic\centi\meter\per\cubic\centi\meter} of
water, which is comparable to the contact probes currently used.

One finding since then has changed the method, and it is worth stating plainly
because it is the sort of thing a reviewer would find first. The index only
cancels the soil underneath if that soil is spectrally flat between the two
wavelengths. Across the \num{23647} air-dry library spectra it is not: for the
outdoor pair the soil-to-soil variation is larger than the entire freezing
signal, so a simple two-wavelength ratio at \num{1200} and
\SI{1250}{\nano\meter} cannot be compared across different soils. Within one
site, as at Poker Flat, it is fine, which is why the result above holds. The fix
is to measure two extra wavelengths either side and subtract the local
baseline, which brings the soil-to-soil variation down to three percent of the
signal, and all four wavelengths sit in the clear part of the spectrum. The
laboratory pair never had this problem. So the outdoor form of the index is a
four-wavelength measurement from now on, and I would rather build that in now
than discover it later.

I have since taken this one step further and made the baseline placement a
result rather than a choice. Searching all candidate baseline windows over one
random half of the \num{23647} library spectra and scoring on the other half
lands within a few nanometres of the windows I had picked by hand, and cuts
the soil-to-soil variation to two percent. The same test shows why the snow
approach cannot simply be reused: a single straight-line background of the
width those methods fit leaves nearly half the freezing signal behind.

\section{Which temperatures matter}

Ground temperature records tell us where to make the measurement. Using the
station \SI{32}{\kilo\meter} from Poker Flat and the three instrumented sites in
interior Alaska over 2017 to 2026, the ground is below freezing on roughly half
of all days and reaches \SI{-27.5}{\degreeCelsius} in midwinter. Taken across
the whole year, only \SI{37}{\percent} to \SI{58}{\percent} of that sub-zero
time is warmer than \SI{-5}{\degreeCelsius}. Restricting to the autumn
freeze-up raises it, though by less than I first thought and by different
amounts at different sites: \SI{50}{\percent} at Bonanza Creek,
\SI{47}{\percent} at Healy, and \SI{91}{\percent} at Delta Junction. The
station nearest Poker Flat, which is snow-insulated and reaches only
\SI{-5.6}{\degreeCelsius}, sits at the warm extreme with essentially all of its
sub-zero time inside the range.

This sets the scope, and it sets it more tightly than the station alone would
suggest. The measurement addresses roughly half of the sub-zero time at two of
the three sites during freeze-up, and most of it at the third. That is still the
right half to target: the freezing curve matters most during freeze-up and thaw,
when the liquid fraction is actually changing and latent heat is being released.
By midwinter the soil has reached its residual unfrozen water content and the
curve is flat, so there is little left to measure. The transition seasons are
also the only part of the cold season with enough sunlight at this latitude for
any future airborne or satellite measurement. The laboratory work and any
eventual remote measurement therefore point at the same window.

\section{The method}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/workflow.png}
\caption{The workflow. Field cores and fabricated specimens feed the two
instruments, each specimen is measured at a series of temperatures rather than
one, and the ladder steps are spaced according to how long real ground spends at
each temperature. The percentage annotated in the figure is superseded by the
values in the text, which come from the full instrumented archive.}
\label{fig:workflow}
\end{figure}

Two instruments divide the work. In Alaska the PSR+ 3500 was used at canopy
level under sunlight, which is why the field spectra carry atmospheric noise. In
the laboratory the same instrument takes a contact probe with its own lamp,
covers \SIrange{350}{2500}{\nano\meter}, and gives a clean spectrum from a
single spot. The HySpex system covers
\SIrange{400}{1000}{\nano\meter} at \SI{1.2}{\nano\meter} and
\SIrange{960}{2500}{\nano\meter} at \SI{1.7}{\nano\meter}, and produces an image
rather than a point. Both instruments cover all three wavelength pairs.

The imaging capability is worth emphasising. Ice does not form uniformly in
soil. It segregates into lenses and layers, and a point measurement averages
over that. An image lets us see whether the liquid fraction varies across a
specimen surface, and whether it varies in a way that matches the visible
cryostructure.

Each specimen is measured at a series of temperatures rather than at one. The
steps are spaced according to how much time real ground spends at each
temperature, so they cluster between \SI{0}{\degreeCelsius} and
\SI{-2}{\degreeCelsius}, thin out to \SI{-5}{\degreeCelsius}, and include a few
colder points to represent midwinter and exposed tundra. A thermocouple in the
specimen gives the temperature, and a contact measurement gives the unfrozen
water content that the optical retrieval is checked against. Which contact
method to use is an open choice, since time domain reflectometry, calorimetry
and magnetic resonance are all accepted, and its uncertainty between
\SI{0}{\degreeCelsius} and \SI{-5}{\degreeCelsius} sets the bar the optical
method has to clear. \textbf{(Need Dr.\ Baser's suggestion as we go.)}

One procedural point is already settled by the December baseline scans. Frost
forming on the specimen surface changes the spectrum noticeably, so it must be
removed immediately before each scan. Our archived field spectra show the same
problem from the other direction.

\section{Using the samples we already have}

The samples collected at Poker Flat last year are at SLU.
They have PSR+ spectra taken in the field under sunlight, full laboratory
characterisation, and no HySpex measurements at all. Rescanning them indoors
would give the first clean shortwave spectra of this material. Scanning them would give a direct comparison between
the two instruments on identical material, spatial maps of the phase partition,
and a second measurement on specimens whose properties are already known. None

Before designing around them I need to know how many are still in usable

It would also help to know which horizons the measurement most needs to work

\section{Plan for scanning now}

Scanning can begin immediately, run by Nirab under the supervision of Ajay or
Majwlan at the RSL while we are in Alaska, using the HySpex procedure already
written for the December baseline. Nothing below requires a new protocol.

\subsection{What runs unchanged from the existing procedure}

The December session established the workflow: the SWIR sensor is powered on at
least thirty minutes ahead so the detector reaches its operating temperature, a
white reference and a dark frame are taken before each batch and repeated at the
end, three specimens are scanned per batch, and twelve specimens are handled per
session across four batches. Surface photographs are taken before and after each
scan, and the session log records personnel, times, ambient temperature and
humidity, every white reference, and any frost seen on a surface. All of this
carries over without change.

\subsection{The one change: unfrozen first}

The December session scanned specimens frozen on dry ice. For the stored Poker
Flat samples we should start with room-temperature scans instead, for three
reasons. It removes the frost problem entirely, so the first data are clean. It
gives the direct comparison against the PSR+ field spectra, which were also
taken unfrozen. And it establishes each sample's dry and unfrozen baseline,
which the index needs as its reference point before any freezing run. Freezing
runs follow once we are back and the ladder is set up.

\subsection{What is needed from Dr.\ Baser's lab}

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item Access to the stored Poker Flat samples.
\item Confirmation of their condition and whether any have degraded or dried out

\subsection{Scanning order}

Scan in the order that answers the most questions soonest. First the samples
that already have both PSR+ spectra and full laboratory characterisation, since
these give the cross-sensor comparison immediately. Then the remaining natural
samples by depth, working from the organic surface layer down through the
mineral active layer to the ice-rich transition. Then the twelve December
specimens rescanned unfrozen, which ties the natural and fabricated sets
together. Recording specimen mass and room temperature at each scan costs
nothing and makes every measurement usable later.

\section{Where I will need help}

This work sits between soil physics, permafrost science and remote sensing, and
several of the choices ahead are outside my own expertise. I have flagged them
in the relevant places above rather than listing them all here.

One of them I have since been able to settle myself, and it is worth recording.
I was worried that freezing changes the way light scatters independently of the
ice content, which would imitate the signal. From the published measurements
that effect turns out to be small. What is not small is the change in how far
light travels through the water film, since ice expands and forms lenses, and
that enters the index exactly like a change in phase. That is what the third
wavelength at \SI{1650}{\nano\meter} is for, and measuring three wavelengths
rather than two also lets the disagreement between them diagnose the problem
when it occurs.

The ones I still cannot answer alone are whether remolded specimens tell us
anything about intact permafrost with its lenses and layering, which horizons
the measurement most needs to work for, what the geotechnical community accepts
as the reference measurement for unfrozen water, and whether the curve shape we
fit suits peat at all. \textbf{(Need Dr.\ Iwahana's and Dr.\ Baser's guidance as
we go.)}
